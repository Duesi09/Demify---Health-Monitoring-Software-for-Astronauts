# Demify research base

Fact-checked research for the Demify master brief, 14 November 2026. Seven themes; each was researched, then attacked by an independent fact-checker. At the end, a read-only map of the Project-2 code.

How to read it: the researcher's facts come first. The fact-check list follows; where it says **wrong** or **corrected**, the fact-check wins. Facts not in the fact-check list were not independently re-checked. Treat "low" confidence and "unconfirmed" as leads, not facts.

## Light-time contracts: pre-approved care plans, computed veto windows and stale-reply handling for delayed crew medical operations

NASA's EIMO work says medical authority must move step by step from Earth to the crew as distance grows, and NASA is building a clinical decision support system for when the ground is "delayed or unavailable". Demify's light-time contracts fit that direction. I could not retrieve the 2024 EIMO ConOps (NTRS 20240001402) itself, so Demify must not quote autonomy levels from it. The strongest backing is behavioural: Mosier & Fischer (Human Factors 2023) found that delay protocols kept crew-to-ground communication about as effective as with no delay. Their protocols label conversation threads and stamp each message with its send time and the earliest time a reply can arrive. The test branch's light-time ledger already does the timestamp part, so contracts only add plan versions, step classes, a computed veto deadline and staleness checks. Present it as making existing practice explicit, computed and audited: ISS medical checklists and flight rules, weekly private medical conferences, and JPL uploading about two weeks of commands before each Mars solar conjunction. Never claim "first". Use a small FHIR-aligned JSON plan format checked with Zod, not CQL or CDS Hooks. The demo moment: switching from Moon to Mars live turns the same pre-approved step from "act unless vetoed" into "act now", because Earth can no longer answer before the clinical deadline.

### Facts (researcher)

- Levin et al. 2023 (IEEE Open Journal of Engineering in Medicine and Biology, 'Enabling Human Space Exploration Missions Through Progressively Earth Independent Medical Operations (EIMO)', doi 10.1109/ojemb.2023.3255513) say Earth-based medical authority must shift progressively to space-based assets as distance grows. Ground teams keep their pre-mission roles while onboard decisions move to the crew. Care and responses to unexpected events fall more and more to the crew as delays and dropouts become routine. ([source](https://doi.org/10.1109/ojemb.2023.3255513); snippet-only; high)
- The same EIMO paper describes the current ISS setup: a ground flight surgeon and deputy on call around the clock, and a crew medical officer (CMO) who gets only a few days of training before launch. ([source](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10810302/); snippet-only; medium)
- A NASA EIMO workshop overview (Easter et al., NTRS 20230016189) says the EIMO team is developing a clinical decision support system to assist the CMO when ground support is delayed or unavailable. Workshop recommendations feed an EIMO Concept of Operations and later updates to NASA-STD-3001. ([source](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20230016189); snippet-only; medium)
- The AsMA 2024 EIMO panel (NTRS 20230015500) summarised Technical Interchange Meetings on data architecture, training, medical supply chain and AI-assisted task-load management. A 2023 npj Microgravity paper is titled 'The value of a spaceflight clinical decision support system for earth-independent medical operations'. Neither gives a public autonomy-level scheme that I could retrieve. ([source](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230015500/downloads/AsMA_2024_EIMO_Panel.pdf); snippet-only; medium)
- A NASA levels-of-care presentation (NTRS 20170000500) puts Level of Care V under the Mars expedition mission type: the physician-astronaut is self-sufficient and relies on the ground only for consults. It lists a 22-minute one-way delay and states 'Limited real-time telemedical consultations for information and guidance = autonomy'. ([source](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20170000500); snippet-only; medium)
- The ISS CMO is not necessarily a physician; any crewmember can be trained as CMO. Reported CMO training hours conflict: about 15 h in total over the two years before launch (NASA AsMA 2021 abstract), about 40 h over 18 months (secondary news site), and 34 h in an old textbook table. A second or backup CMO per expedition is unconfirmed. ([source](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20205008925); snippet-only; medium)
- A NASA study of former ISS CMOs (IWS, NTRS 20230013415) found they saw the role as a 'healthcare extender' to ground flight surgeons. For exploration missions with limited ground contact, they wanted a physician CMO and more rigorous training. ([source](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20230013415); snippet-only; medium)
- ISS flight surgeons hold weekly private medical conferences (PMCs) with each astronaut. A 2017 NASA presentation describes them as weekly 1-on-1 sessions of 15 minutes or more, with extra ones in the first and last weeks. On the ground, the flight surgeon (board-certified physician) oversees crew medical concerns and a biomedical engineer console supports medical equipment and operations. ([source](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20170001693/downloads/20170001693.pdf); snippet-only; medium)
- The ISS Medical Checklist has separate emergency and non-emergency sections and is used alongside a video, email and phone link to the ground medical team. A 2011 NASA effort rewrote more than 120 checklist procedures, with on-board implementation planned for June 2011; the exact 2011 NTRS document carrying this was not pinned down. The medical flight rules text itself was not found publicly. ([source](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20110003477); snippet-only; low)
- Kintz & Palinkas (Aerospace Medicine and Human Performance 87(11):940, 2016) ran an ISS delay study: 10 tasks, 6 with no delay and 4 with a 50-second one-way delay, with individual and team performance rated after each task. ([source](https://asma.kglmeridian.com/downloadpdf/view/journals/amhp/87/11/article-p940.pdf); snippet-only; medium)
- Fischer & Mosier (2014, lab): teams took significantly longer to repair system failures under time delay than without delay. ([source](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20140004216/downloads/20140004216.pdf); snippet-only; medium)
- Mosier & Fischer, 'Meeting the Challenge of Transmission Delay: Communication Protocols for Space Operations', Human Factors 65(6), 2023 (online 2021). Their protocols reduced threats to common ground. In NEEMO and HERA, compliance was high and perceived crew-to-ground communication effectiveness under delay stayed comparable to no-delay days. Untrained crews rated delay days considerably lower. Recommendations: label communication threads, and record each message's send time and the earliest time a reply can be expected. NASA began adopting the latter via an automated text system. ([source](https://phys.org/news/2015-07-nasa-protocols-time-delay.pdf); secondary; high)
- NEEMO 13 summary finding: simple information should be sent by a simple method such as text (less bandwidth, less 'nuanced interpretation'). A NASA report found text messaging reduced both flight-controller and crew workload under delay. NEEMO 20 used 5-minute one-way delays (per a vendor blog, secondary). ([source](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20120007707/downloads/20120007707.pdf); snippet-only; medium)
- Palinkas & Chou (NEEMO 15/16): longer delay was associated with more crew autonomy, but not significantly. A NASA 2025 HRP workshop review of 20 years of delay research says delays harm behavioural health, performance and space-to-ground team cohesion. It says high crew autonomy is well received in analogs, but the effect is confounded with the delay. It also reports that delay lowered message-routing completion in HERA, and that flight-controller workload rose with delay (attributed to a Kanas et al. 2011 Mars-500 105-day study with 0 or 20-minute delay; attribution not fully confirmed). ([source](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20250000703/downloads/2025%20HRP%20IWS%20Comm%20Delay.pdf); snippet-only; medium)
- SIRIUS-19 (Russia, published 2021, not Mars-500): under autonomous conditions crews underwent psychological 'autonomization' and became less dependent on mission control. ([source](https://www.frontiersin.org/news/2021/11/09/isolation-mars-communication-sirius-russia); secondary; medium)
- CHAPEA Mission 1 lasted 378 days (June 2023 to July 2024), with mission control enforcing a communication delay of up to 22 minutes one way. No published Mission 1 autonomy results were found. Mission 2 crew entered the habitat on 19 October 2025 (single secondary source). ([source](https://texasstandard.org/stories/nasa-chapea-mars-simulation-crew-emerges-texas/); secondary; high)
- Light time: Moon about 1.3 s one way. Mars about 3 to 22 min one way, depending on orbital position; sources give upper bounds from 21 to 24 min. Computed from the repo's 0.37 to 2.67 AU: 184.6 s (3.1 min) to 1332 s (22.2 min). ([source](https://spaceacademy.net.au/spacelink/commdly.htm); secondary; high)
- Mars solar conjunction comes about every 26 months. NASA pauses commanding to Mars spacecraft for about two weeks (e.g. 2 to 16 Oct 2021; 11 to 25 Nov 2023) and uploads about two weeks of commands beforehand, which is the robotic precedent for pre-approved plans. A NASA ASCEND 2022 paper on crewed Mars missions says disruptions can last from several days to several months, depending on how sensitive the radio band is to the Sun. ([source](https://www.space.com/nasa-mars-blackout-solar-conjunction-2021); secondary; high)
- HL7 CPG-on-FHIR (current 2.0.0, STU2) defines computable guidelines on top of FHIRPath, CQL, CDS Hooks and SMART. CQL is a superset of FHIRPath. CDS Hooks is aimed at clinician-facing remote decision support inside an EHR. The CDS Hooks Service PlanDefinition profile is Trial Use, maturity level 0. ([source](https://www.hl7.org/fhir/uv/cpg/methodology.html); snippet-only; high)
- The WHO SMART Guidelines L3 authoring guidance maps L2 processes and decision tables to FHIR PlanDefinition, activities to ActivityDefinition, and calculation logic to CQL libraries. Each PlanDefinition has a logic library. L2, the human-readable decision-table layer, is the format a clinician reviews. ([source](https://smart.who.int/ig-starter-kit/v1.0.0/l3_decisiontables.html); snippet-only; high)
- FHIR PlanDefinition lifecycle fields: status (draft, active, retired, unknown), with a version required for non-experimental active artifacts. Also approvalDate, lastReviewDate and effectivePeriod; date must change when the business version or status changes. Several instances can share an identifier with different versions; knowledge-artifact versioning is deferred to CRMI guidance. ([source](https://www.hl7.org/fhir/R4/PlanDefinition.html); snippet-only; high)
- Existing test-branch code: src/domain/consult.ts computes earliestAnswerS = roundTripS + handlingTimeS, with handling of 5 s live or 600 s by message (both illustrative). src/domain/missionContext.ts has a 5-step autonomy scale with round-trip thresholds of 1 s, 10 s and 300 s, plus no contact, labelled 'ours, not NASA's'. It sets BLACKOUT_DAYS = 14 and has five profiles: iss, moon, transit, mars-far, blackout. ([source](https://github.com/ (repo branch origin/test, files src/domain/consult.ts and src/domain/missionContext.ts)); primary; high)

### Recommendations

- STEP CLASSES (the flight surgeon authors one per step). (1) ACT_NOW, 'act now, report after': time-critical, self-contained steps, e.g. go to storm shelter, stop exercise, lie down, recheck vitals in 10 min. They run at once and the report goes out in the next message; Earth can only review them, not veto. (2) ACT_UNLESS_VETOED, 'act unless vetoed by T': the crew announces the step, which runs automatically at T_exec unless a veto has arrived, e.g. cancel tomorrow's EVA, start a fluid-loading protocol, move a crewmember to a light-duty schedule. (3) GROUND_APPROVAL, 'needs ground approval, with blackout fallback': irreversible or mission-affecting steps such as clearing someone back to EVA, using a scarce consumable or changing the care plan. The step waits for explicit approval; if there is no approval by the clinical deadline, or the link is down, it runs the fallback named in the plan (default: hold, keep monitoring, and escalate the decision to commander plus CMO). (4) INFORM: logged and reported only. Drug doses never appear in any class.
- EFFECTIVE CLASS IS COMPUTED, NOT ONLY AUTHORED. effectiveClass = f(authoredClass, T_veto, D_clin, contact). If an ACT_UNLESS_VETOED step's T_veto is later than its clinical deadline D_clin, it becomes ACT_NOW only if the plan sets allowEscalateToActNow; otherwise it becomes GROUND_APPROVAL with fallback. When there is no contact, ACT_UNLESS_VETOED steps follow the same rule and GROUND_APPROVAL steps go straight to fallback. Show both labels on screen, e.g. 'Authored: act unless vetoed. Today: act now (Earth cannot answer before the 30-min deadline)'.
- VETO DEADLINE FORMULA (put it in src/domain/consult.ts next to earliestAnswerS and reuse milestones()). tau(t) = distance_km(t) / 299792.458, computed per leg. t_earth = t_send + wait_downlink + tau. t_reply_leaves = max(t_earth + H_ground, next_uplink_window). T_veto = t_reply_leaves + tau + M. Use margin M = max(60 s, 0.05 x round trip) and H_ground = 600 s by message (the current constant) or 5 s on live voice; label both assumptions. T_exec = clamp(max(T_veto, step.earliestStart), ..., D_clin). The ground console shows its own deadline: 'send veto by T_exec - tau (Earth UTC)'. Worked values (labelled as illustrative): Mars farthest, 22.2 min one way: T_veto = send + 44.4 + 10 + 2.2 = about 56.6 min. Mars nearest, 3.1 min: send + 6.2 + 10 + 1 = about 17.2 min. Moon: send + 2.6 s + 5 s + 60 s = about 68 s. The wait_downlink and next_uplink_window terms default to 0 (continuous contact) but make the same model work for Antarctic or maritime contact windows.
- STALE REPLIES. Every outgoing message carries threadId, a per-thread sequence number (Mosier and Fischer thread labelling), planId, planVersion, the plan content hash, stateSeq (ship-state counter, incremented on every new finding, executed step or plan change), sentAt and earliestReplyAt. Every reply carries inReplyTo and basedOnStateSeq. On arrival, flag STALE if basedOnStateSeq < current stateSeq or planVersion differs. Show a diff, e.g. 'Written on data up to 13:58; since then: step 2 executed, heart rate 112 to 96'. A veto that arrives after T_exec is shown as 'LATE VETO: step already done at 14:41; ground asks to stop or reverse'. It is never silently dropped and never auto-reverses.
- LOST MESSAGES. Earth sends an automatic receipt; expect it by t_send + 2 tau + 60 s. Without one, resend once and mark 'receipt unconfirmed'. A step that runs at T_exec with no receipt is logged as 'executed under pre-approval; Earth had no opportunity to veto'. Plans can set requiresReceipt: true for ACT_UNLESS_VETOED steps; such steps then wait until D_clin and take the fallback. Treating silence as consent (the UNODIR convention) is legitimate only once receipt is confirmed; say that out loud in the pitch.
- BLACKOUT (solar conjunction, about 14 days, BLACKOUT_DAYS = 14 already). Add a pre-blackout 'contract review' screen, modelled on JPL uploading two weeks of commands before conjunction. It lists every active plan whose approval expires during the blackout, every GROUND_APPROVAL step and its fallback, and a re-sign action for the flight surgeon. During blackout, show 'Earth cannot veto for N days; acting under plans approved on <date>'. After the link returns, the first downlink is the flight-recorder delta for Earth to review.
- PATIENT IS THE MEDIC. Mark the CMO in crew data. When the patient is the CMO, or when the reaction self-test (PVT-B style, test branch) or vitals show impairment, switch to ASSIST MODE: steps are addressed to 'helper', and ACT_UNLESS_VETOED and GROUND_APPROVAL steps need a second crew member's confirmation before they run. If the patient is alone, show only steps marked selfAdministrable: true and add 'self-treatment, unverified' to the flight recorder.
- PLAN FORMAT. Use your own small JSON schema, validated with Zod, whose field names mirror FHIR R4 PlanDefinition and ActivityDefinition so a later export is mechanical. Fields: id, version (semver), status (draft, active or retired), approvedBy, approvalDate, effectivePeriod {start, end}, lastReviewDate, contentHash (SHA-256 of the canonical JSON), trigger (finding ids or threshold rule ids from the existing engine), and action[] with {id, title, text, stepClass, clinicalDeadlineMin, earliestStartMin, fallback, selfAdministrable, requiresReceipt, allowEscalateToActNow, relatedAction [{actionId, relationship: 'after-end', offsetMin}]}. Plans are immutable: any edit creates a new version, needs re-approval and keeps the old version. Only status=active within effectivePeriod can run. Do NOT adopt CDS Hooks: it assumes an online EHR calling a remote service, and Demify is offline. Do NOT build a CQL engine now; reuse the existing TypeScript rule engine and its tagged thresholds. For flight-surgeon review, render each plan as a WHO SMART L2-style decision table (trigger, step, class, deadline, fallback). Say 'FHIR-aligned', not 'FHIR-compliant'.
- FLIGHT RECORDER (extends the append-only audit table on test). One row per event: plan id, version, hash (8 hex shown), step id, authored and effective class, rule id and rule version that fired, input values, the computed tau, H, M, T_veto, T_exec and D_clin, receipt status, actor (CMO, helper or auto), executedAt, and on any reply its staleness verdict. Expose it read-only at /api/v1/crew/{crewId}/audit, which already exists.
- 60-SECOND DEMO. 0 to 10 s: pick 'Mars, farthest'; the header shows one way 22.2 min, round trip 44.4 min, earliest Earth answer about 54 min. 10 to 25 s: trigger a scenario (NOAA proton-flux spike from a snapshot, or tachycardia plus dizziness). The contract card 'SPE-01 v1.2, approved <date> (example plan)' opens: Step 1 Shelter now [ACT NOW]; Step 2 Recheck vitals in 15 min [ACT NOW]; Step 3 Cancel tomorrow's EVA [ACT UNLESS VETOED, veto deadline 15:47 ship time, counting down]; Step 4 Resume exercise [NEEDS GROUND, fallback: stay restricted]. 25 to 35 s: switch to 'Moon'; Step 3's veto window drops to about 1 min, then switch back. On Mars, with a 30-min clinical deadline, Step 3 turns into ACT NOW with the reason shown. 35 to 45 s: fast-forward until a simulated reply arrives with a red STALE badge ('based on 13:58 data, 2 steps executed since'). 45 to 52 s: 'Behind the Sun': Step 4 shows its fallback, banner 'No veto possible for 14 days; plans re-signed before blackout'. 52 to 60 s: open the flight recorder row for Step 3 (rule, version, hash, T_veto, who). Closing line: 'Earth keeps oversight; the crew never waits on the speed of light.'
- PITCH WORDING (honest). 'Light-time contracts: the flight surgeon approves care plans before an emergency, the crew acts at once, and Earth keeps a veto window computed from the live light time.' 'This builds on how spaceflight already works: ISS medical checklists and flight rules, weekly private medical conferences, and JPL loading two weeks of commands before solar conjunction. What we add is making the timing explicit, computed and audited.' 'Inspired by NASA's Earth-Independent Medical Operations research; not affiliated with or endorsed by NASA.' 'Example plans were written by our team and have not been reviewed by a flight surgeon.' 'Research prototype, not a certified medical device.' Cite the behavioural basis as: 'NASA-funded studies (Mosier and Fischer, Human Factors 2023) found that labelling threads and stamping messages with the earliest possible reply kept crew-ground communication effective under delay; we built that into the product.'
- PILOT METRICS for Asclepios VII or MDRS with simulated delay (follow the Mosier and Fischer method): time from finding to first action; perceived communication-effectiveness ratings on delay versus no-delay days; stale replies caught; late vetoes; steps run with receipt unconfirmed; and how often the effective class differed from the authored class. These give a measurable pilot outcome for ESA BIC or Venture Kick reviewers.
- Before the pitch, keep the existing 5-step autonomy scale but map it to the classes on screen. Level 3 'You start, the ground steers' is where ACT_UNLESS_VETOED is meaningful. Levels 4 and 5 are where effective classes start escalating. Keep the label 'the steps and their limits are ours, not NASA's'.

## Expected Self: time-resolved normal spaceflight adaptation per Demify indicator, and how to turn it into an expected-band model

Only body mass has strong enough evidence to move the band (25 ISS crew, two devices: about -3 to -4 % in the first 30 days, then flat). Core temperature (about +1 °C over about 2.5 months, one study) and blood pressure (-8 to -10 mmHg over 24 h, 8 men, and contradicted by a study of 6 with no change) can only move it at half weight, with an "early evidence" label. Sleep (about 6.1 h on the ISS), insulin resistance, early slowing in reaction time and a fall in aerobic capacity are well documented but harmful, so Demify should name them as typical and still flag them; "expected" must never mean "acceptable". Resting heart rate (unchanged), HRV (mixed), SpO2 (no in-flight data found), mood (third-quarter effect not supported) and SANS or vision symptoms (risk grows with mission day, so never hide them) must stay plain baseline or absolute comparisons. For the pitch, the strongest honest line is: "Demify rates you against what spaceflight normally does to you on mission day N. Each curve shows its study and sample size, and clinical red limits are never relaxed." Note that the realistic 2027 users (analog crews at 1 g) must run with the microgravity curves turned off.

### Facts (researcher)

- Core body temperature (Stahn et al. 2017, Sci Rep 7:16180): resting core temperature rose about 1.0 °C (reported 1.01 °C) above pre-flight during ISS missions. The rise built up gradually over about 2.5 months and went with higher IL-1 receptor antagonist. During exercise, core temperature rose higher and faster than on Earth. It was measured with a non-invasive forehead sensor, not an oral or ear thermometer. This is a single study; the sample size was not confirmed in this run. ([source](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5701078); primary; high)
- Exercise core temperature about 1.4 °C higher than on Earth, with some astronauts above 40 °C: this comes from press coverage only and could not be confirmed in the primary paper. ([source](https://www.sci.news/space/weightlessness-body-temperature-05609.html); secondary; low)
- Blood pressure (Norsk et al. 2015, J Physiol 593(3):573-584): 24-hour ambulatory brachial blood pressure fell by about 8-10 mmHg. Cardiac output and stroke volume rose 35-41 % between months 3 and 6 on the ISS, more than on shorter flights. Systemic vasodilatation is the likely cause. Sample: 8 men aged 45-53, so a single small, all-male study. The time course before month 3 was not confirmed. ([source](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/4324706/); primary; high)
- Contradicting the blood-pressure finding, Hughson et al. 2012 (J Appl Physiol, n=6) found no significant change from pre-flight to in-flight in heart rate, blood pressure or baroreflex. After landing, heart rate rose 5-6 bpm and cardiac output rose 0.6 L/min. The URL is the CSA project page for the CCISS experiment that produced the paper; the numbers come from search snippets only. ([source](https://www.asc-csa.gc.ca/eng/sciences/cciss.asp); snippet-only; medium)
- Resting heart rate is largely unchanged in flight. In Fraser et al. (IAC 2011 conference abstract, n=7, 24-hour Holter), night heart rate was 55.5±7 bpm pre-flight and 55.7±8 bpm in flight. Daytime heart rate was lower in flight, which the authors linked to less physical activity. Otsuka et al. (n=10, ECG) report resting heart rate held at pre-flight values. ([source](https://iafastro.directory/iac/archive/browse/IAC-11/A1/2/9471/); primary; medium)
- Heart rate variability (Xu et al. 2013, Am J Physiol Regul Integr Comp Physiol 305(2):R164-70, n=7, up to 6 months on the ISS): HRV during sleep fell, partly explained by movement during sleep (slow fluctuations below 0.04 Hz). Complexity and fractal dynamics did not change. Other ISS data (Otsuka, n=10) show a larger circadian heart-rate amplitude in space, with parasympathetic activity reported as both decreased and increased depending on the study. Overall the HRV evidence is mixed. ([source](https://www.sciencegate.app/document/10.1152/ajpregu.00423.2012); secondary; medium)
- Sleep (Barger et al. 2014, Lancet Neurol): 21 ISS astronauts over 3,248 in-flight days averaged 6.09 h of sleep, and shuttle crews 5.96 h, against the 8.5 h NASA schedules. Only 24 % of ISS sleep episodes and 12 % of shuttle episodes lasted 7 h or more. 12 of 16 ISS crew used sleep medication. Sleep was already short in the 3 months of pre-flight training; after the flight, 7 h-plus episodes rose to 42 % and 50 %. ([source](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4188436); primary; high)
- Sleep timing (Flynn-Evans et al. 2016, npj Microgravity, doi 10.1038/npjmgrav.2015.19): in 21 long-duration ISS astronauts, sleep episodes out of step with the body clock were linked to worse sleep and more medication use. Causes include slam shifts, non-24-hour schedules and badly timed light (the cupola window, tablet use before bed). The exact durations were not retrieved in this run. ([source](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20160003864); primary; medium)
- Body mass (Zwart et al. 2014, PubMed 25197887): in 25 ISS crew measured on both on-board devices, mean loss was -4.4 % on the Russian BMMD and -2.8 % on NASA's SLAMMD. The loss happened in the first 30 days; body mass then stayed constant for the rest of the mission. In-flight food intake was about 80 % of the WHO requirement, and mass tracked intake. The two devices differed by 1.1 kg on average. Body mass and food intake are measured weekly in flight so the flight surgeon can adjust intake. A review puts typical loss at 2-5 %, with more than 5 % on earlier missions. ([source](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/25197887); primary; high)
- Glucose and insulin (Hughson et al. 2016, Am J Physiol Heart Circ Physiol 310:H628-H638, not CMAJ): an in-flight insulin-resistance index rose (P<0.001), more in men. Fasting insulin rose in all astronauts. Fasting glucose rose in men but not women. After the flight, carotid stiffness was 17-30 % higher. This is a single study; the sample size and effect sizes were not confirmed. Lee et al. 2020 found no significant change in carotid stiffness. ([source](https://works.bepress.com/kevin-shoemaker/56); secondary; medium)
- Aerobic capacity (Moore et al. 2014, J Appl Physiol 117(3):231): VO2peak on flight day 15 (not 14) was 17 % below pre-flight (95 % CI -22 % to -13 %). It rose slowly during the flight (0.001 L/min per day) but did not return to pre-flight. On the first day after landing it was 15 % below pre-flight, and within 2 % by day 30 after landing. Sample: 14 astronauts (9 men, 5 women), 91-192 days. Individuals varied widely: some showed no substantial change. ([source](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2014/08/140829115607.htm); secondary; medium)
- Cognition (Dev et al., Front Physiol, November 2024, doi 10.3389/fphys.2024.1451269): 25 astronauts on 6-month ISS missions took the 10-test Cognition battery, which includes the 3-minute PVT-B. Scores were corrected for practice effects and compared with each astronaut's pre-flight baseline. Early in flight, processing speed, visual working memory and sustained attention were slower. Attention slowing was seen only early in the mission; processing speed did not return to baseline until after it. Accuracy did not fall, and there was no systematic decline. No millisecond magnitudes were retrieved. ([source](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fphys.2024.1451269/full); primary; high)
- The Reaction Self-Test study collected PVT-B, sleep and alertness data from 24 astronauts on 6-month ISS missions. The full Cognition battery took 16 minutes on average on the ISS. Published PVT-B results in milliseconds or lapses were not found in this run. ([source](https://www.med.upenn.edu/uep/astronaut.html); primary; medium)
- Third-quarter effect: Kanas et al. followed 13 astronauts and 17 cosmonauts (Shuttle/Mir and ISS, 4-7 month missions) with weekly surveys and found no substantial change in mood or interpersonal measures in any quarter. Their 2021 review of more than 30 years of work found little evidence of a time-dependent third-quarter effect. Mars-500 showed no third-quarter effect on psychological measures. ([source](https://asma.kglmeridian.com/downloadpdf/view/journals/amhp/92/8/article-p689.pdf); primary; high)
- Hawkes and Norris (Polar Record 2017) meta-analysed 21 Antarctic studies with 1,826 participants: negative mood did not peak at the third quarter. Some single studies did report a third-quarter rise, for example Steel 2001. ([source](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/S003224741700050X); primary; high)
- SANS: optic disc edema graded on the Frisén scale appears in about 16-19 % of long-duration crew, and OCT-measured disc edema in about 70 %. Nearly all astronauts show some SANS-spectrum change in eye structure. Optic nerve head rim tissue and peripapillary choroid thicken from early flight, with the maximum near the end of the mission (around +35.7 µm rim and +43 µm choroid at flight day 150 in a 6-month cohort). In Mader 2011, 7 astronauts after 6 months showed disc edema (5), globe flattening (5), choroidal folds (5) and cotton-wool spots (3). Choroidal folds can persist for years. ([source](https://scholarlycommons.henryford.com/surgery_articles/493); secondary; medium)
- Fluid shift: on entering weightlessness about 2 L of fluid moves from the lower to the upper body, and total blood volume then falls. Plasma volume falls about 10-15 % in flight (review). No early-flight rise and later normalisation of intraocular pressure could be confirmed; the evidence on IOP is mixed. Leg-volume figures were not found. ([source](https://www.nasa.gov/sites/default/files/files/Experiment_Summary_Fluid_Distribution.pdf); primary; medium)

### Recommendations

- Implement three classes per indicator in one versioned data file (for example src/lib/expected/curves.json, logged by version in the flight recorder). (1) SHIFT, where the expected band moves: body mass, core temperature, blood pressure. (2) CONTEXT, where Demify shows 'typical in spaceflight' but keeps every threshold: sleep duration and timing, fasting glucose and insulin, Reaction Self-Test and cognition, exercise capacity and exercise heart rate. (3) BASELINE, with no curve: resting heart rate, HRV, SpO2, mood and stress, hydration, vision and SANS symptoms, radiation dose.
- Model: expected(d) = personal pre-flight baseline + w × Δ(d), where Δ is a piecewise-linear curve of [mission day, shift] and w is the evidence weight. Tolerance = max(2 × pre-flight median absolute deviation, the literature spread). z = (value - expected) / tolerance: caution at |z| ≥ 2, alert at |z| ≥ 3. Evaluate absolute NASA-STD-3001 and clinical (NEWS2, AHA, ADA) limits FIRST and never relax them. The expected band may turn a 'caution' into 'typical adaptation', never an 'alert' into anything less.
- Evidence weight w: 1.0 when at least 2 independent studies or n ≥ 20 agree (body mass); 0.5 for a single study or one contradicted by another (core temperature, Stahn; blood pressure, Norsk n=8 men against Hughson 2012 n=6, no change); 0 when evidence is absent or mixed (resting heart rate, HRV, mood, SpO2), which means plain baseline. Show a badge on every rating, for example 'Evidence: 1 study, 8 men' or 'Evidence: 25 ISS crew'.
- Body mass curve: Δ = -3 % × min(d/30, 1), tolerance ±2 % (the evidence range is 2-5 %). Because mass normally stops falling after day 30 (Zwart 2014), flag any continued downward trend after day 30 and any total loss above 5 %. Describe both as Demify design rules, not NASA limits. Insist on the same scale before and during flight: the two ISS devices differ by 1.1 kg on average.
- Core temperature curve (resting only): Δ = +1.0 °C × min(d/75, 1), with w = 0.5, so the expected plateau is +0.5 °C until better data exist. Never apply it to exercise readings. Keep the NEWS2 temperature bands absolute, so a reading of 38.1 °C or above is still at least caution whatever the curve says. Note in the UI that Stahn measured with a forehead heat-flux sensor, so oral readings may not match.
- Blood pressure: do not move the upper limit. Widen only the lower caution margin by up to 10 mmHg from day 30, so in-flight vasodilation is not flagged as hypotension. Label it 'single study, 8 men aged 45-53'. Keep the AHA high-pressure thresholds absolute.
- Add a post-landing phase to the mission timeline. Expect heart rate +5 to 6 bpm right after landing (Hughson 2012, n=6; how long this lasts is unconfirmed). Expect exercise capacity -15 % on the first day after landing, back within 2 % by day 30 (Moore 2014, n=14). Show this as context, not a shifted band.
- Sleep: keep the target at 7 h or more, and show 'ISS crews average 6.1 h (Barger 2014, 21 astronauts): common, not healthy'. Add a 'sleep out of step with your body clock' flag when the sleep window moves by more than a set amount from the pre-flight habitual window (Flynn-Evans 2016 links this to worse sleep and more medication). The exact threshold is a design choice.
- Reaction Self-Test: before taking a baseline, require at least 3 pre-flight practice sessions, because Dev 2024 corrected for practice effects. In the first weeks of flight, show 'early-flight slowing in processing speed and sustained attention is typical (Dev 2024, 25 astronauts)', but keep the go/no-go lapse rule unchanged. No published millisecond shift exists to justify relaxing it.
- Mood: remove any automatic 'third-quarter' warning. If the concept appears, word it as 'often cited, not supported in ISS, Mir, Mars-500 or Antarctic meta-analysis data'. Use personal baseline plus absolute screening thresholds.
- Vision and SANS: risk grows over the mission (disc edema in 16-19 % by fundus grading, about 70 % by OCT), so treat any new near-vision change or headache symptom as at least caution, at every mission day. Never write 'expected, ignore'. Demify does not measure intraocular pressure or the optic nerve.
- Make the curves depend on the mission profile. Microgravity curves apply only to ISS and transit profiles. Analog profiles (Asclepios, MDRS: 1 g) and Moon or Mars surface profiles (partial gravity, no data) run with the curves off, plain baseline, plus the sleep-timing and isolation context. Say in the pitch that the first real users, analog crews, run in baseline mode, and that Expected Self is demonstrated on a simulated ISS crew.
- Store one record per curve with these fields: indicator, profile, points [[day, delta]], unit, tolerance, weight, source citation, DOI, n, sex mix, grade, version. The flight recorder then logs 'rule vX + Expected-Self curve vY' for every piece of advice.
- Pitch wording: 'Demify does not page the flight surgeon for what spaceflight does to everybody; it pages for what spaceflight should not be doing to you.' Follow it with the honest scope: 'three indicators shift, four are put in context, the rest are compared with your own baseline, and every curve shows its study and sample size.'

### Fact-checker objections to the recommendations

- SAFETY, downgrade rule: 'the expected band may turn a caution into typical adaptation' must exclude every caution raised by a NASA- or Clinical-tagged absolute threshold. Examples: NEWS2 temperature 38.1-39.0 °C scores 1 point, and NEWS2 systolic 101-110 mmHg scores 1 point. Only cautions from the App personal-baseline rule may be reclassified. Otherwise the core-temperature curve, with resting core temperature near 38 °C in flight, would silence NEWS2 fever cautions. Write it as an engine invariant and add a unit test.
- SAFETY, blood pressure: widening the lower caution margin from day 30 has no evidence behind it. Norsk measured at months 3-6; Hughson 2012 (6 men) found no in-flight change, and both studies were all-male. Use weight 0 (plain baseline), or start no earlier than day 90 with the label 'two small all-male studies, conflicting'. Never touch the NEWS2 systolic bands (≤90 scores 3, 91-100 scores 2, 101-110 scores 1); keeping only the AHA high limits absolute is not enough.
- Core temperature: the primary paper reports exercise core temperature above 40 °C in some astronauts and a peak about 1.39 °C higher than on the ground. Exercise readings should therefore get a heat-strain caution, as a Demify design rule, not just be left out of the curve. Cite the primary paper instead of calling the finding press-only. Apply the curve only to readings from the same kind of sensor, a core or forehead heat-flux sensor; label oral and ear readings 'curve not applicable'.
- Post-landing phase: drop '+5 to 6 bpm' because it is unconfirmed; the Hughson 2012 abstract says only 'relatively small postflight changes at rest while seated'. The Moore 2014 figures (-15 % on the first day after landing, within 2 % by day 30) are confirmed and can stay as context.
- Body mass: the mean difference between the two devices is 1.1 kg, about 1.5 % of a 75 kg astronaut, which uses up most of the ±2 % tolerance. Require the same device and reject cross-device comparisons. Zwart 2014 is one study (25 crew measured on 2 devices), not two independent studies, so it qualifies for weight 1.0 only through the n ≥ 20 rule. Do not say the loss is caused only by eating too little: fluid loss may contribute (published letter and reply).
- Model maths: tolerance = max(2 × MAD, spread) combined with caution at |z| ≥ 2 means caution fires only at about 4 MAD, which is much less sensitive than the existing rule that a 25 % shift from baseline raises caution. Pick one definition, document it and test it on the demo crew before the pitch.
- SANS: replace 'disc edema in 16-19 % of crew' with 'Frisén grade 1 edema in about 17 % of retinal images (62 crew); OCT shows disc edema developing in about 70 % of 6-month ISS crew'. Remove all µm figures, because the studies that measure them disagree.
- Pitch line 'Demify does not page the flight surgeon for what spaceflight does to everybody' contradicts the researcher's own CONTEXT and SANS rules: short sleep, insulin resistance and SANS changes affect most crew and are still flagged. Use instead: 'Demify shows which changes are typical on mission day N and still pages for anything past clinical limits or outside your expected band.'
- 'Rates you against what spaceflight normally does to you' overclaims personalisation. The curves are group means from samples of 6 to 25 people, mostly men, applied to your own pre-flight baseline. Say 'what spaceflight typically does to crews on mission day N, starting from your own baseline'.
- Reaction Self-Test: the rule of 3 or more pre-flight practice sessions is a Demify design choice, not something Dev 2024 prescribes. Label it as Demify's rule.
- Mood: cite only Hawkes and Norris 2017 (confirmed) for the third quarter. The Kanas 2021 review and Mars-500 claims are unconfirmed. Also, negative mood was highest at the START of deployment, so an early-mission mood caution is better supported than a third-quarter one.

### Do not claim

- That 'space fever' or a 38 °C core temperature is normal: the rise is about +1 °C at rest in one study, and fever thresholds stay in force.
- A Stahn 2017 sample size, or the 'exercise 1.4 °C higher / above 40 °C' figures, as confirmed: they are press-only and unconfirmed.
- That blood pressure falls 10 mmHg in all astronauts: the source is 8 men, and Hughson 2012 (n=6) found no in-flight change.
- That resting heart rate drops in space: the evidence says it is largely unchanged; only daytime, activity-related heart rate is lower.
- That HRV reliably decreases in flight: the evidence is mixed (sleep HRV lower in n=7; parasympathetic activity reported both up and down).
- The third-quarter effect as an established phenomenon.
- That Demify detects or measures SANS, intraocular pressure or optic disc edema.
- That intraocular pressure rises early in flight and then normalises: unconfirmed.
- Any in-flight SpO2 value for ISS crews: none was found in this run.
- Hughson 2016 as a CMAJ paper (it is in Am J Physiol Heart Circ Physiol), or a sample size or effect size for its insulin-resistance result.
- That arterial stiffness equals '10-20 years of ageing' as fact: it is the author's commentary, and Lee et al. 2020 found no significant change.
- Moore 2014 measured at 'flight day 14' (it was flight day 15).
- Dev et al. as a 2025 paper (it was published November 2024).
- A millisecond magnitude for in-flight PVT-B slowing.
- The Flynn-Evans '63 % vs 49 %' figure: its context is unconfirmed.
- That Expected Self predicts illness, or that it is validated on astronaut data: it is a literature-derived research prototype.
- That Demify is the first to model expected adaptation.
- That the Stahn exercise result (>40 °C) is press-only: it is in the paper. Also do not say temperatures 'often' exceeded 40 °C; the paper says 'some individuals'.
- Any Stahn 2017 sample size: still unconfirmed.
- That the blood-pressure evidence includes women: Norsk (8) and Hughson 2012 (6) were all men.
- Hughson 2016 sex differences as established: the sample was n=8 (4 men, 4 women), so describe it as a signal only.
- 'Optic disc edema in 16-19 % of crew': the confirmed figure is 17.35 % of images from 62 crew.
- Any µm values for optic nerve or choroid thickening.
- A post-landing heart-rate rise of +5-6 bpm, or cardiac output +0.6 L/min.
- That in-flight body mass loss is caused only by eating too little, or the 80 % of WHO intake figure.
- Barger post-flight percentages swapped: it was 42 % shuttle, 50 % ISS.
- That intense in-flight exercise has been proven to prevent loss of aerobic capacity: this is the authors' argument from n=14.
- That the third-quarter effect was disproved in astronauts by Kanas 2021 or Mars-500: unconfirmed in this run; cite only the Antarctic meta-analysis.
- That Expected Self curves are personalised predictions: they are group means added to a personal baseline.

### Open questions

- Stahn 2017: sample size, measurement time points and how much individuals differed (needed to set the temperature tolerance).
- Norsk 2015: blood-pressure values before month 3 and the standard deviation of the 24-hour drop; whether systolic and diastolic were reported separately.
- Hughson 2016: number of astronauts and size of the HOMA-IR and fasting-glucose changes.
- Published PVT-B results in milliseconds or lapses from the Reaction Self-Test study (24 ISS astronauts), and the time course of early-flight slowing in days.
- In-flight SpO2 data for ISS crews, and whether reduced-pressure, raised-oxygen exploration cabins would change expected SpO2 (unconfirmed).
- Post-landing heart-rate elevation: how many days it lasts.
- Whether any curves exist for partial gravity (lunar surface, Mars), where no data are known.
- Data for women: Norsk's sample was all men and Hughson found differences between the sexes, so curves may need to depend on sex.
- Time course of intraocular pressure and leg-volume loss; quantified fluid-shift data from the first few days.

## Environment and radiation context, real data sources, and explainable cause attribution ("since the solar particle event", "since your sleep shifted")

Demify's core numbers hold up against primary sources: the 1.8 mSv/day Mars cruise rate (Zeitlin 2013), the 600 mSv career effective-dose limit for all ages and sexes (NASA-STD-3001, based on 3% REID for a 35-year-old female), the S-scale's ≥10 MeV / 10 pfu threshold, the 3 mmHg one-hour CO2 limit (plus a 7-day target under 2.5 mmHg) and the PVT-B rules (3 min, 1–4 s gaps, 355 ms lapse). The strongest radiation line for the pitch is that the cruise alone on a Mars round trip comes to about 0.66 ± 0.12 Sv (Zeitlin 2013). That is already more than the 600 mSv career limit. Solar particle events added only about 5% of the cruise dose, so most of the dose is background cosmic radiation and the crew cannot avoid it. Two things must change before the demo. First, the NASA DEMO_KEY allows only 30 requests per hour and 50 per day per IP address, and a shared hackathon network will hit that. Use a registered key on the server plus the offline snapshots. Second, every "since X" explanation should be a rule with a version: one exposure, a time window, the indicators it affects and a level of evidence. Wording should be "coincides with" or "possibly related", never "caused by". The UI should also show competing explanations, for example CO2 versus the headward fluid shift for a headache. Nothing could be fetched from this sandbox: the NOAA, NASA and PubMed hosts are blocked. Evidence therefore comes from search snippets plus the repository's own snapshots, which show the NOAA SWPC endpoints answered on 2026-11-14 at 08:52 UTC with ≥10 MeV flux near 0.2 pfu (quiet) and a 5% chance of S1 for 14–16 November.

### Facts (researcher)

- Zeitlin et al., Science 2013 (MSL RAD instrument during the cruise to Mars): average galactic cosmic ray dose equivalent rate about 1.8 mSv/day (1.84 is the more precise figure). Shortest round trip with current propulsion: 0.66 ± 0.12 Sv. Solar particle events contributed only about 5% of the cruise dose. ([source](https://www.swri.org/newsroom/press-releases/swri-led-team-calculates-the-radiation-exposure-associated-trip-mars); primary; high)
- NASA-STD-3001 career limit: 600 mSv effective dose, the same for all ages and sexes. National Academies 2021: derived by applying the NSCR-2012 cancer model to a 35-year-old female so that mean REID (risk of exposure-induced death from cancer) is 3%. The committee supported a single dose-based standard but recommended NASA re-examine whether 3% REID is the best metric. The exact revision of Volume 1 that adopted it was not confirmed. ([source](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/26155/chapter/5); primary; high)
- ISS dose: a NASA-affiliated review gives effective dose accumulating at roughly 0.2 to 0.5 mSv or more per day. Crew badges usually read absorbed dose below 0.3 mGy/day over 20 years, at times above 0.4 mGy/day. DOSIS 3D measured 195–360 µGy/day absorbed dose in Columbus, depending on solar cycle and altitude. Demify's own OSDR EDA snapshot gives mean ISS absorbed dose 0.31 mGy/day (GCR 0.15 + SAA 0.16). Computed: 180 days at 0.2–0.5 mSv/day is about 36–90 mSv, or 6–15% of the career limit. ([source](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10919966); secondary; medium)
- During EVA the effective dose equivalent rate approaches 700 µSv/day, about 20% above intravehicular activity (MATROSHKA phantom). Personal dosimeters overestimate effective dose by about 15% inside the station and by more than 200% during EVA. Absorbed dose (mGy) and effective dose (mSv) must never be added together. ([source](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4206298/); primary; medium)
- Crew Active Dosimeter (CAD): developed by Mirion with NASA JSC's Space Radiation Analysis Group. Used on the ISS since 2020, replacing the legacy passive crew detectors. Uses direct ion storage, logs time-stamped dose continuously and sends it by Bluetooth to station laptops. Worn by each Artemis II astronaut (Mirion press release of 2026-03-23; the mission returned 10 April 2026). Reference paper: Life Sciences in Space Research 2023, 'The importance of time-resolved personal dosimetry in space: The ISS Crew Active Dosimeter', PMID 37945094. ([source](https://www.mirion.com/discover/knowledge-hub/articles/stories/from-ram-to-cad-the-evolution-of-astronaut-radiation-monitoring-through-artemis-ii); primary; medium)
- NOAA S-scale (solar radiation storms) uses ≥10 MeV integral proton flux at geosynchronous orbit (GOES), on a logarithmic scale: S1 ≥10 pfu, S2 ≥100, S3 ≥1,000, S4 ≥10,000, S5 ≥100,000. A storm begins when ≥10 MeV flux reaches 10 pfu. Astronaut wording seen only on third-party copies (official NOAA text not confirmed): S3 'radiation hazard to astronauts on EVA', S4 'high', S5 'unavoidable high radiation hazard to astronauts on EVA'. ([source](https://glossarystaging.ametsoc.net/wiki/S-scale); secondary; high)
- SWPC JSON endpoints listed under services.swpc.noaa.gov/json/goes/primary/ (listing still current in June 2026): integral-protons-6-hour, -1-day, -3-day and -7-day.json; xrays-6-hour, -1-day, -3-day and -7-day.json; xray-flares-latest.json; xray-flares-7-day.json. A /json/goes/secondary/ mirror exists for failover. Integral proton channels: ≥1, ≥5, ≥10, ≥30, ≥50, ≥60, ≥100 and ≥500 MeV. X-ray passbands: 0.1–0.8 nm (flare class) and 0.05–0.4 nm. Demify also uses /products/noaa-scales.json and /products/noaa-planetary-k-index.json, and its snapshots show all four answered on 2026-11-14 at 08:52 UTC. No schema documentation or rate limit was found. ([source](https://services.swpc.noaa.gov/json/goes/primary/); primary; high)
- SWPC and NOAA terms: information on government servers is in the public domain and may be used freely (archived 2012 SWPC page). NOAA policy asks for attribution and forbids implying NOAA endorsement or presenting altered content as official. NOAA's open-data policy puts federal environmental data in the US public domain. The current SWPC wording was not confirmed. ([source](https://ospo.noaa.gov/Organization/About/use.html); primary; medium)
- NASA api.nasa.gov (includes DONKI): DEMO_KEY allows 30 requests per hour and 50 per day per IP address. A free registered key defaults to 1,000 requests per hour. Exceeding a limit blocks the key, and the block lifts after an hour. Every response carries X-RateLimit-Limit and X-RateLimit-Remaining headers. DONKI paths: /DONKI/FLR, /SEP, /CME and others, with startDate/endDate as yyyy-MM-dd and api_key. ([source](https://api.nasa.gov/assets/html/authentication.html); primary; high)
- The CCMC also serves DONKI web-service calls on kauai.ccmc.gsfc.nasa.gov/DONKI/WS/get/{CME, CMEAnalysis, GST, IPS, FLR, ...}. Dates default to 30 days before the current UTC date through today. Not confirmed: whether an SEP route exists on that host, whether no key is needed, and whether an acknowledgment is required. The web-service API was announced on 2022-09-01. ([source](https://ccmc.gsfc.nasa.gov/tools/DONKI); primary; medium)
- NASA OSDR has two APIs. (1) The OSDR Developer API (REST) does full-text search, metadata retrieval and file retrieval, e.g. /osdr/data/osd/files/{n} with comma-separated multi-study requests; Demify also uses /osdr/data/search and /geode-py/ws/... routes. (2) The Biological Data API at visualization.osdr.nasa.gov/biodata/api, with /v2/query/metadata/ and /v2/dataset/... paths. OSDR is also in the AWS Open Data Registry as a public S3 bucket. RadLab is described as a NASA tool for exploring environmental data, but no RadLab API documentation turned up in search (the repo cites visualization.osdr.nasa.gov/radlab/gui/data-api). No rate limit or attribution rule was found. ([source](https://osdr-tutorials.readthedocs.io/en/latest/pages/guides/biological_data_api.html); primary; medium)
- Cabin CO2: NASA-STD-3001 Vol 2 (Rev D per the OCHMO brief), requirement V2 6004, limits the average 1-hour ppCO2 in the habitable volume to 3 mmHg or less. The OCHMO technical brief says the 7-day average must stay below 2.5 mmHg to keep headache risk under 1%, and gives the current ISS range as 1–9 mmHg. It also notes that the microgravity fluid shift (reduced venous and lymphatic drainage) causes symptoms similar to CO2 over-exposure, including headache. ([source](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-004-carbon-dioxide.pdf); primary; high)
- Law et al., J Occup Environ Med 2014 (ISS crew reports 2001–2012): the odds of a reported headache doubled for each 1 mmHg rise in CO2. Weekly headache risk fell from about 3.3% to 1.6% once CO2 was held at 4 mmHg or less (press coverage). ([source](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2014/05/140501100922.htm); secondary; high)
- Barger et al., Lancet Neurology 2014: 85 astronauts (64 on 80 Shuttle missions, 21 on the ISS). ISS sleep averaged just over 6 h. Sleep episodes of 7 h or more happened on only 24% of ISS nights. Sleep medication use was widespread (the exact percentages vary across summaries, so cite the paper, not the figures). ([source](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20140810/Space-missions-affect-sleep-patterns-in-astronauts.aspx); secondary; medium)
- Flynn-Evans, Barger, Kubey, Sullivan, Czeisler, 'Circadian misalignment affects sleep and medication use before and during spaceflight', npj Microgravity 2016, doi 10.1038/npjmgrav.2015.19. Operations make crews work in the biological night and sleep in the biological day; misalignment went with shorter sleep and more medication. The paper's exact operational definition of a misaligned sleep episode was not confirmed. ([source](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20160003864); primary; high)
- PVT-B (Basner, Mollicone, Dinges, Acta Astronautica 69, 2011): validated in 74 healthy adults under total and partial sleep deprivation. It shortened the 10-min PVT to 3 min and the interstimulus interval from 2–10 s to 1–4 s. The lapse threshold was lowered from 500 ms to 355 ms, and with it the PVT-B was as sensitive to sleep loss as the 10-min PVT on every outcome. PsyToolkit notes the paper is ambiguous about the interval and one review says 2–5 s. Repeated administration shows no systematic learning effect. ([source](https://www.med.upenn.edu/uep/assets/user-content/documents/Basner2011-ValidityandsensitivityofabriefPVT.pdf); primary; high)
- PVT false starts are commonly defined as a response with no stimulus or a reaction time under 100 ms (standard PVT practice). This was not confirmed for the PVT-B paper in this run. Demify's 5,000 ms no-response timeout is a project default, not a published value. ([source](https://www.psytoolkit.org/experiment-library/pvtb.html); snippet-only; low)
- HRV monitoring best practice (Australian Institute of Sport guide): use lnRMSSD, judge 7-day rolling averages against a smallest worthwhile change (SWC); waking and nocturnal measurements are both valid. The SWC band is commonly ±0.5 SD around the baseline (attributed to Plews et al. 2012; seen only in secondary sources). Plews: at least 3 random days per week gives results equivalent to a full 7-day average. A different published method calls a drop significant when a daily value is 1 SD or more below a 10-day baseline, or it falls on 2 consecutive days. Do not mix the two methods. These are sports-science methods, not spaceflight standards. ([source](https://www.ais.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0013/1130260/HRV-BPG_FINAL.pdf); primary; medium)
- Precedent: an open FHIR 'aerospace-medicine-ig' implementation guide (awatson1978, version 0.5.12 ci-build) has a radiation-tracking page and gives 0.3–0.5 mSv/day for low Earth orbit. It is a software specification, not a dosimetry source. ([source](https://awatson1978.github.io/aerospace-medicine-ig/radiation-tracking.html); snippet-only; medium)
- Demify code check (origin/Project-2-Phase-1): estimateDoseRate adds 0.01 mSv/day per pfu above 1 pfu, times a 0.05 factor in LEO. These coefficients have no source and must be tagged 'App'. Mission baselines: ISS 0.5, Artemis 1.3, Mars transit 1.8 mSv/day. knowledge.ts says 'lunar ≈ 1.4' while config says 1.3, so the two disagree. ([source](https://github.com/ (repository file src/lib/sources/swpc.ts and src/lib/config.ts on branch Project-2-Phase-1)); primary; high)

### Fact-checker objections to the recommendations

- Dose-budget pitch line 'the cruise comes to about 660 mSv, already more than the career limit' overstates. Zeitlin's 0.66 ± 0.12 Sv is the dose equivalent a detector measured behind MSL shielding at low-to-moderate solar activity, while the 600 mSv limit is effective dose, and the uncertainty range goes below 600. Fix: 'The transit alone to Mars and back is about 660 mSv dose equivalent (Zeitlin 2013), comparable to NASA's 600 mSv career limit.'
- R-SLEEP-1 wording 'Reactions slower since your sleep moved about 3 h later … This pattern has been observed in spaceflight' misattributes the evidence. Spaceflight data (Jones 2022) tie PVT-B deficits to sleep of 6 h or less, not to a shift in sleep timing. Flynn-Evans 2016 ties misalignment to shorter sleep and more sleep medication. Fix: set the duration trigger at 6 h or less (matching Jones), say 'observed in spaceflight' only for the duration rule, and tag the 2-hour timing-shift trigger as an App assumption.
- Naming: the test branch calls its browser test 'Reaction Self-Test', which is the name of NASA's own ISS tool (Reaction Self Test with the 3-min PVT-B, Jones 2022). A judge could read that as equivalence or a NASA tool. Rename it (e.g. 'Reaction check') with the subtitle 'modelled on the PVT-B used in NASA's Reaction Self Test'.
- CO2 rule: cite the 7-day < 2.5 mmHg / < 1% headache figure to the OCHMO brief only. Coverage of Law 2014 gives < 2 mmHg for < 1%, so the 2.5 mmHg figure must not be attributed to Law. - Lunar baseline: drop the suggested '1.3–1.4' range. Use 1.4 mSv/day (Chang'e-4 measured 1,369 µSv/day dose equivalent on the surface, Science Advances 2020, doi 10.1126/sciadv.aaz1334) and label it 'lunar surface'. config.ts's 1.3 has no source.

## Regulation, ethics and privacy for a Swiss open-source astronaut health-monitoring prototype (MDR/MedDO, HRA, revFADP/GDPR, AI Act, CRA, PLD, licence, trademark, NASA branding)

The pitch branch has three problems to fix before Sunday. Its wording, "Decision support, not a medical device", names the very function that makes software a medical device under MDR Rule 11. A chest-pain action names a drug (aspirin, src/lib/health/knowledge.ts line 538). And there is no LICENSE file, even though the project is pitched as open source (the test branch is MIT). Being free or open source does not take Demify out of the MDR. What keeps today's prototype out is that it is not supplied commercially and its stated intended purpose is research and training; that statement includes the slides, README and on-screen text. The most valuable near-term step is the Asclepios VII call for projects, which closes on 29 November 2026 and asks that ethics approval already be requested. Design the study as an HRO Chapter 2 category A study (ethics only, Ticino committee), not as a ClinO-MD category C device investigation (Swissmedic plus ethics). The rule-based engine is outside the EU AI Act definition of an AI system; the CRA and the new Product Liability Directive exclude open-source software supplied outside a commercial activity, but both will catch Demify once it is sold; a Swiss trademark costs CHF 350 online for 3 classes.

### Facts (researcher)

- MDR Annex VIII Rule 11: software that provides information used to take diagnostic or therapeutic decisions is class IIa. It is IIb if those decisions could cause serious deterioration of health or a surgical intervention, and III if they could cause death or irreversible deterioration. Software that monitors physiological processes is IIa, and IIb for vital parameters whose variations could put the patient in immediate danger. All other software is class I. For Demify: check-in rating plus flight-surgeon alert is IIa or higher, vital-sign alarms are IIb, and a heart-attack escalation could be argued to be III. ([source](https://blog.johner-institute.com/regulatory-affairs/mdr-rule-11/); secondary; high)
- MDR Art. 2(12) defines intended purpose as the use for which a device is intended according to the manufacturer's label, instructions for use, promotional or sales materials or statements, and clinical evaluation. Pitch slides, the README, the website and UI text therefore all count towards qualification. ([source](https://advisera.com/13485academy/mdr/definitions/); secondary; high)
- MDR Art. 2(27)/(28): 'making available' is any supply for distribution, consumption or use on the market 'in the course of a commercial activity, whether in return for payment or free of charge'; placing on the market is the first making available (investigational devices excepted). Being free is NOT an exemption; the deciding question is whether there is a commercial activity. ([source](https://advisera.com/13485academy/mdr/definitions/); secondary; high)
- MDR recital 19: software for general purposes (even in a healthcare setting) or for life-style and well-being is not a medical device. Software specifically intended for a medical purpose is. ([source](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2017/745/oj); primary; high)

## How to win: 2026 Space Apps rules, judging, timeline, past winners, competing teams on the same challenge, and the pitch plan

Judges score five criteria, Impact, Creativity, Validity, Relevance and Presentation, each from 1 to 5, and no weights are published. Business is not a criterion, so frame the business part as the path to impact and give it about 45 seconds of a 5-minute pitch. Global judging looks only at the project page and its demo: a video of 30 seconds or less, or up to 7 slides. Anything over that limit is ineligible. Nominees are announced on 3 December 2026, finalists on 17 December and the 10 winners in January 2027, so the submission must make the case without the live pitch. At least 15 public 2026 repositories tackle the same challenge, and several already have the features Demify would otherwise call unique: delay-aware escalation and an audit trail (VITA), a personal baseline, cited rules, the 600 mSv limit, PVT-B and NOAA data (AURORA), a hash-chained ledger (RAKIA) and a 'black-box flight recorder' (VitalGrid). I found no public entry with care plans the flight surgeon approves in advance plus an Earth veto window computed from the live light time, none using real Inspiration4 crew biomarkers, and none with a go/no-go for a specific task. Those three should carry the pitch. The 2025 Best Use of Science winner, SpaceGenes+, was an astronaut-health dashboard built on molecular science for a named user (researchers and mission planners), so NASA rewards real data, a specific user and an honest scope.

## Competitor fact-check: the systems a judge is most likely to name, and one judge-proof sentence each on how Demify differs

The two closest rivals both already run offline. NASA/Google CMO-DA is an AI diagnostic assistant: early tests on simulated cases scored 74 to 88 % physician-rated accuracy, and since June 2026 it has run offline (Red Hat RamaLama) on a ground twin of the ISS Spaceborne Computer, but it has not been deployed in space. The Booz Allen/Oura/Axiom Ax-4 demo (June 2025) processed ring data on an ISS edge device, with no Oura cloud, to give crew a readiness view for critical tasks. So Demify must never claim to be first at offline astronaut AI, crew readiness, or personal baselines (Apple Vitals and Oura already compare users to their own baseline). The claims that hold up are a published source and rule version behind every threshold (traceable, unlike an LLM answer), escalation that adapts to the signal delay, one explanation across body, symptoms and radiation, open code, and acceptance of readings from any device. That makes Astroskin, ARCHeR actigraphs, Oura rings and Cardi/o radar possible inputs rather than rivals. On regulation, Oura, Apple and Cardi/o call themselves wellness, not medical. The FDA sent WHOOP a warning letter in July 2025 over a blood-pressure feature and closed it in June 2026. Biofourmis shows this category can win FDA 510(k) clearance, which supports keeping the wording "research prototype, not a certified medical device".

### Facts (researcher)

- CMO-DA (NASA JSC with Google Cloud, announced Aug 2025): AI clinical decision support meant to help crew diagnose and treat symptoms without a link to Earth. Early tests on simulated cases gave physician-rated diagnostic accuracy of 88 % for ankle injury, 80 % for ear pain and 74 % for flank pain (figures attributed to NASA presentations). It was described as an early research system. ([source](https://www.techspot.com/news/109014-nasa-google-developed-ai-medical-assistant-keep-astronauts.html); secondary; medium)
- The 2025 CMO-DA proof of concept was reported as running on Google Cloud Vertex AI, which is cloud-based. One outlet says it uses open models such as Llama 3 and Mistral Small; that model claim is unconfirmed. ([source](https://dev.aiwire.net/2025/08/19/google-and-nasa-pilot-an-ai-medical-assistant-for-deep-space/); secondary; medium)
- June 2026 (Red Hat blog 24 June, The Register 27 June): CMO-DA now runs fully offline. Red Hat's open-source RamaLama packages the AI models as containers, tested on the terrestrial twin of the HPE Spaceborne Computer that is on the ISS. It is not deployed on the ISS. The next step is a demo to NASA leadership after ground validation, with RHEL AI planned as the runtime. TechRadar headlined it as an 'open source medical system'. No regulatory clearance and no use of vital-sign streams or a personal baseline was reported. ([source](https://www.redhat.com/en/blog/moon-and-beyond-ramalama-being-tested-nasa-potentially-support-medical-ai-assistant-future-deep-space-missions); snippet-only; high)
- ARCHeR (Artemis II human research): crew wear wrist actigraphy devices that track motion, sleep and light exposure. Data collection runs from about 6 months before launch to about 1 month after return. The study looks at individual and team performance, including movement inside Orion. NASA says the monitors let crew and mission control study real-time health and behavioural information for crew safety. It is a research study, not a product. No regulatory status or flight results were found. ([source](https://www.nasa.gov/humans-in-space/artemis-ii-crew-to-advance-human-spaceflight-research/); primary; high)

([source](https://universemagazine.com/en/wrist-device-monitored-the-astronauts-health-during-the-flight-to-the-moon/); secondary; low)
- Ax-4 'ISS Edge Computing for Crew Readiness Demonstration' (ISS National Lab-sponsored; Booz Allen, Axiom Space, Oura): Booz Allen changed the Oura ring middleware so that data went to a disconnected cloud device on the ISS instead of Oura's ground servers. An edge computer processed the data in near real time and gave it directly to the crew member to judge readiness for critical tasks. Oura's normal setup sends ring data to Oura's cloud. Ax-4 was scheduled for no earlier than 10 June 2025 (actual launch date not confirmed here). No published results were found. ([source](https://axiomspace.com/research/iss-edge-computing-for-crew-readiness-demonstration); primary; high)

## Project-2 code map

Project-2-Phase-1 (HEAD 0017e6b, "Make drills visible everywhere and alarms activity-aware") is a single Next.js 16.4.6 App Router app (React 19.3, TS strict, Tailwind 4.3, Recharts 3, Zod 4, Vitest 5, output: standalone, Docker node:24-alpine). It shares no code with the test branch: the two diverged at 6dddc02 "README Update", so porting means adapting code, not merging. Layers: (1) src/lib/types.ts is the domain model: 20 IndicatorIds, 5 hazards, 9 body regions, Reading with source manual|sensor|simulated|osdr|noaa-swpc, Scenario, LiveVitals, MonitorAlarm. (2) src/lib/health/knowledge.ts holds the rules: INDICATORS (piecewise steps, basis NASA|CLIN|APP, escalateAbove/Below, actions, sources), HAZARDS, BODY_REGIONS, classify/evaluateValue. src/lib/health/assess.ts is the server-only engine. (3) src/lib/sources/ holds data adapters with one fallback chain (cache.ts): swpc.ts (NOAA), osdr.ts (Inspiration4 OSD-569/575, study search, mission registry), eda.ts (OSDR Environmental Data App, ISS dosimetry), simulator.ts (deterministic vitals, daily check-in stories, live samples, drill overlays). (4) src/lib/store/ has readings.ts (JSON file demify-db.json, fsync and atomic rename, retention 1100 days, cap 50k readings, 4 default crew cdr/plt/ms1/ms2 with pre-flight baselines) and scenarios.ts (drills on globalThis plus a JSON mirror, auto-end after 30 min). (5) src/lib/monitor/ has live.ts (instant vitals, sensor values newer than 2 min override simulated ones) and alarms.ts (IEC 60601-1-8-style high/medium limits, activity-aware for exercise and sleep). (6) Pages, all force-dynamic: / Overview, /crew (redirects 307 to a member), /crew/[id] (patient monitor with synthesised ECG/pleth, health record, findings, emergency drills and checklist), /body, /check-in (server action submitCheckIn), /radiation, /research (NASA Data), /guidance. A global AlarmCenter in layout.tsx polls /api/alarms every 2 s and plays alarm sound. (7) API routes: /api/readings GET+POST (Zod, 1 MB cap, 600 readings/min per crew member, cross-origin check), /api/assessment, /api/crew, /api/health, /api/space-weather, /api/vitals/live, /api/alarms, /api/scenarios GET/POST/DELETE, /api/osdr/{inspiration4,search,mission,eda}. There is no auth anywhere. The CI file .github/workflows/ci.yml runs npm ci, lint, test and build (DEMIFY_OFFLINE=true), then a docker build with an offline smoke test. ### Assessment engine

Flow per crew member, assess.ts assessCrewMember() lines 243-334. The engine pulls 7 days of readings with readingsFor() (stored readings, 24 h of simulated 10-min series, simulated daily check-ins and one live sample at now). For each of the 20 INDICATORS:
(1) currentValue() (assess.ts:71). Future-dated readings are dropped. A real reading beats a simulated one; for continuous signals only while it is less than 6 h old. Drill samples (note 'scenario:*') are used as is. Continuous signals take the median of the last 60 min excluding note==='exercise', with a fallback to the last 6 resting readings. Everything else takes the latest value.
(2) evaluateValue() (knowledge.ts:589). It rounds to the indicator's decimals, then classify() checks the ascending steps. Personal baseline rule: if the status is nominal, the indicator is continuous and |value-baseline|/baseline >= 0.25, the status becomes caution (baselineShift=true). escalate = status==='critical' OR the value is past escalateAbove/escalateBelow.
(3) trendFlag() (assess.ts:138), on one value per UTC day: mood drop >= 2 within the previous 3 days gives caution; stress rise >= 2 gives caution; sleep < 6 h on each of the last 7 days gives critical (and escalates).
(4) Radiation (assess.ts:288). With no dosimeter value, the dose rate comes from the NOAA estimate. S1-S2 raises to at least caution, S3+ to critical. Any S>=1 escalates, but only on fresh data; a stale snapshot only adds a 'verify with dosimeters' note.
(5) finding() text gives the value, the % change against pre-flight when at least 5 %, and 'within normal range' / 'outside normal range, monitor closely' / 'large change from your baseline' / 'critical, act now'. Actions are the indicator's actions when status is not nominal.
(6) Roll-up: each region takes the worst status of its indicators, overall takes the worst of all, and escalations = all assessments with escalate set.
There is NO cross-indicator pattern logic (no 'SpO2 down + CO2 up = cabin air' and similar), no drift over a mission, no explanation of causes, no versioning of rules and no record of advice. Separate from this engine, alarms.ts raises beat-to-beat monitor alarms. Examples: HR >120 medium / >150 high, or >170/185 during exercise; SYS >160/>180, <90/<80; SpO2 <92/<88; RR >28 (45 during exercise), <=4 apnea; ST >=1 mm high; PVC >=6/min. activeProtocol() maps alarms to checklists.
