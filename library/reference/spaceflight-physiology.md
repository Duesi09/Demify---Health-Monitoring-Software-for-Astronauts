# Spaceflight physiology

How the body changes in spaceflight, as summarised in the Demify health knowledge base. Training reference for a research prototype; the flight surgeons stay the authority.

## The five hazards (NASA HRP, RIDGE)

- Radiation: cancer risk, effects on the central nervous system and cognition, degenerative disease of the heart, blood vessels and eyes. A solar particle event can cause acute effects.
- Isolation and confinement: sleep loss, disrupted circadian rhythm, anxiety and depression, conflict within the crew.
- Distance from Earth: about 3 to 22 minutes one-way signal delay to Mars, depending on planetary positions, so the crew must be medically self-sufficient.
- Gravity fields: bone loss of 1–1.5 %/month, loss of muscle and fitness, SANS (eye changes from fluid shift), orthostatic intolerance (fainting after return to gravity), kidney stones.
- Hostile closed environment: CO₂, microbes, immune changes, noise and light.

## Fluid shift and the heart

Microgravity shifts fluid headward and the heart becomes more spherical; arrhythmia risk rises and orthostatic tachycardia or fainting is common after landing. The heart becomes rounder and does less work; blood volume drops, causing low blood pressure and fainting at landing. 24-hour blood pressure drops about 8–10 mmHg in flight as fluid redistributes (Norsk 2015). Before landing the crew follows the pre-landing fluid-loading procedure and wears compression garments. Core temperature rises up to about 1 °C over long missions (impaired heat loss without convection, Stahn 2017), so compare against your own in-flight baseline.

Sources: NASA HRP, Risk of Cardiovascular Adaptations (https://www.nasa.gov/reference/risk-of-cardiovascular-adaptations/); NASA OCHMO-TB-019 Orthostatic Intolerance (https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-019-orthostatic-intolerance.pdf).

## Vision and SANS

The headward fluid shift causes Spaceflight Associated Neuro-ocular Syndrome (SANS): optic disc swelling, choroidal and retinal folds and a flattened eyeball. About 70 % of long-duration crews show signs of SANS; 18 % are clinically significant. Cabin CO₂ may contribute to SANS. Eyes are checked with eye charts, OCT, a fundoscope and ocular ultrasound in "eye weeks" (flight day 30, 90, return-30). Crews fly 3 or more strengths of glasses and switch to the next pair of backup glasses when near vision blurs; keep cabin CO₂ low. Any new visual change escalates: OCT and fundoscope exam (with ground support when contact allows). Radiation also raises cataract risk (lens dose limits).

Sources: NASA OCHMO-MTB-001 SANS (https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/ochmo-mtb-001-sans.pdf); NASA HRP, SANS (https://www.nasa.gov/reference/risk-of-spaceflight-associated-neuro-ocular-syndrome-sans/).

## Bone

Without countermeasures weight-bearing bones lose 1–1.5 % per month; the spine lengthens up to 3 %. Released calcium raises kidney-stone risk, and so does dehydration. NASA requires hip and spine bone mineral density (BMD) to stay at or above 95 % of pre-flight. DXA scans happen before and after flight; in flight the loss is estimated from exercise adherence and urine calcium. Daily ARED resistance sessions protect bone.

Sources: NASA OCHMO-MTB-013 Bone Loss (https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/ochmo-mtb-013-bone-loss.pdf); NASA HRP, Spaceflight-Induced Bone Changes (https://www.nasa.gov/reference/risk-of-spaceflight-induced-bone-changes/); NASA-STD-3001 Vol. 1 Rev C.

## Muscle and fitness

Strength and aerobic fitness drop 10–12 % on average without exercise; some crew lose more than 20 % without countermeasures. NASA requires VO₂max and strength to stay at or above 80 % of pre-flight. NASA plans 1.5–2 h of exercise per day, 6–7 days per week, on the ARED resistance device, the T2 treadmill and the CEVIS cycle. Under-eating, menu fatigue and muscle loss reduce body mass; ISS crews typically lose 2–5 %.

Sources: NASA, Astronaut Exercise (https://www.nasa.gov/general/astronaut-exercise/); NASA HRP, Reduced Fitness (https://www.nasa.gov/reference/risk-of-reduced-cardiorespiratory-and-musculoskeletal-fitness/); NASA OCHMO-TB-013 Food and Nutrition.

## Sleep and circadian rhythm

NASA schedules an 8.5 h sleep period, but crews average about 6–6.5 h. Sixteen sunrises a day, noise, CO₂ and workload disrupt circadian rhythm. Less than 6 h of sleep for 7 days is equivalent to 24–36 h without sleep. Protect the full 8.5 h sleep period; bright or blue-enriched light 1–2 h after waking and none 1–2 h before bed; no caffeine 6–12 h before sleep. Poor sleep before critical tasks (docking, EVA, landing) is a reason to contact the flight surgeon.

Sources: NASA OCHMO-TB-041 Sleep (https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-041-sleep-accommodations.pdf); NASA OCHMO-MTB-009 Circadian Desynchrony (https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/ochmo-mtb-009-circadian-desynchrony.pdf); NASA HRP, Inadequate Sleep and Irregular Schedules (https://www.nasa.gov/reference/risk-from-inadequate-sleep-and-irregular-schedules/).
