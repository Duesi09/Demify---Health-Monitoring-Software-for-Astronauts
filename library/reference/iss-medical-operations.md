# ISS medical operations

How medical care is organised on the International Space Station, from public NASA, ESA and press sources. Training reference for a research prototype: Demify is not connected to any ISS system, device or data link, and the flight surgeons stay the authority. Many NASA primary documents could not be opened during the research, so model names and numbers here are the best public information, not official specifications.

## Crew medical officers (CMOs)

ISS medicine is mostly telemedicine. Usually two crew members per crew are trained as Crew Medical Officers (CMOs), a primary and a backup. They are not doctors: flight surgeons on the ground guide the crew medical officers through every medical task.

Sources: NASA, Flight surgeons (https://www.nasa.gov/directorates/esdmd/hhp/flight-surgeons); NASA NTRS 20080010877 (https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20080010877); NASA NTRS 20205008925 (https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20205008925).

## Flight surgeons

The flight surgeon sits at the "Surgeon" console in Mission Control Houston. The flight surgeon runs the private medical conferences, guides diagnosis and treatment, and advises the flight director on medical go/no-go decisions.

Sources: NASA, Flight surgeons (https://www.nasa.gov/directorates/esdmd/hhp/flight-surgeons); NASA, Houston We Have a Podcast: Mission Control crew health consoles (https://www.nasa.gov/podcasts/houston-we-have-a-podcast/mission-control-crew-health-consoles).

## Biomedical engineers (BMEs)

Biomedical engineers (BMEs) are the flight controllers for the medical hardware. They plan the medical procedures and send them up to the station.

Sources: NASA, Biomedical flight controllers (https://www.nasa.gov/directorates/esdmd/hhp/biomedical-flight-controllers/).

## Private Medical Conference (PMC)

Each crew member has a weekly Private Medical Conference (PMC) with the medical team: one crew member at a time, about 15 minutes, and nobody outside the medical team listens. A crew member can ask for an extra conference at any time. What is said there is private medical information.

Sources: NASA, Houston We Have a Podcast: Mission Control crew health consoles (https://www.nasa.gov/podcasts/houston-we-have-a-podcast/mission-control-crew-health-consoles); NASA, Flight surgeons (https://www.nasa.gov/directorates/esdmd/hhp/flight-surgeons).

## Medical procedures with ground guidance

Crews follow step-by-step medical procedures, often with a flight surgeon on the line. The biomedical engineers plan these procedures and send them up. Demify's drill checklists are training material written in the same step-by-step style; they never replace the mission's own procedures, and ground guidance comes when contact allows.

Sources: NASA, Biomedical flight controllers (https://www.nasa.gov/directorates/esdmd/hhp/biomedical-flight-controllers/); NASA medical kit checklist, 2015 public release (https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2015/03/medical_kit_checklist_-_full_release.pdf); ISS medical emergency manual, 2016, as published by Government Attic (https://www.governmentattic.org/19docs/NASA-ISSmedicalEmergManual_2016.pdf).

## CPR and the Crew Medical Restraint System (CMRS)

For CPR the patient is strapped to the Crew Medical Restraint System (CMRS), a board that holds the patient, the rescuer and the equipment. The CMRS also isolates the patient electrically during defibrillation. The station's AED, a Physio-Control LIFEPAK 1000, has been on the ISS since July 2008; public sources do not confirm that it is still the AED in 2026.

Sources: ISS medical emergency manual, 2016 (https://www.governmentattic.org/19docs/NASA-ISSmedicalEmergManual_2016.pdf); Scandinavian Journal of Trauma, Resuscitation and Emergency Medicine, 2020 (https://sjtrem.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s13049-020-00793-y); Health Imaging (https://healthimaging.com/node/32039); Diagnostic and Interventional Cardiology (https://www.dicardiology.com/node/24705).

## Imaging available on the ISS

Ultrasound guided from the ground is the main diagnostic imaging tool on the ISS. Since September 2025 the station has a GE HealthCare Vivid iq ultrasound system, which replaced the Vivid q from 2011; a live video downlink lets specialists on the ground guide the scan. For the eyes (SANS checks) the crew uses optical coherence tomography (OCT, a Heidelberg Spectralis, in orbit since 2013), a fundoscope, eye ultrasound and tonometry, compared with pre-flight scans. Advanced imaging beyond these is not on the station.

Sources: GE HealthCare press release (https://www.gehealthcare.com/en/about/newsroom/press-releases/taking-innovative-technology-to-the-final-frontier-ge-healthcares-vivid-iq-ultrasound-system-launches-to-the-international-space-station); NASA, Ultrasound-2 (https://www.nasa.gov/ames/space-biosciences/ultrasound-2-sts-135/); ESA, Advanced ultrasound (https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Human_and_Robotic_Exploration/International_Space_Station_Benefits_for_Humanity/Advanced_Ultrasound_for_the_Space_Program_and_on_Earth); NASA OCHMO-MTB-001 SANS (https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/ochmo-mtb-001-sans.pdf); Healio, 2014 (https://cm.healio.com/news/optometry/20140217/spectralis-being-used-on-space-station); NASA ISS daily summary, 5 June 2013 (https://www.nasa.gov/blogs/stationreport/2013/06/05/iss-daily-summary-report-06-05-13/).

## What the station cannot do: imaging and laboratory

- No advanced imaging beyond ultrasound and the eye-exam devices: a crew member who needs it has to return to Earth (see Crew-11 below).
- Most laboratory work happens on Earth: blood draws are frozen for return to Earth. On board there are a handheld blood analyser (Abbott i-STAT), urine dipsticks and a centrifuge.
- IV bags cannot drip by gravity: the IV Supply Pack holds a pressure infusor.

Sources: Clinical Chemistry, 2023 (https://doi.org/10.1093/clinchem/hvad035); NASA ISS daily summary, 2 May 2013 (https://www.nasa.gov/blogs/stationreport/2013/05/02/iss-daily-summary-report-05-02-13/); NASA NTRS 20110011351, CHeCS (https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20110011351/downloads/20110011351.pdf); NASA medical kit checklist, 2015 (https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2015/03/medical_kit_checklist_-_full_release.pdf).

## What the station cannot do: data and ground contact

- Very few medical devices send data over a network. Most show a value on a screen or store it on the device or a laptop; results reach the flight surgeons as a file, a voice report or live video.
- Medical inventory tracking is still largely manual: RFID sees whole kits, not their contents, and there is no public inventory interface.
- Contact with the ground has gaps: the Ku-band link through the TDRS relay satellites covers about 85 % of each orbit, with about 600 ms round trip, and its capacity is shared by the whole station.

Sources: Clinical Chemistry, 2023 (https://doi.org/10.1093/clinchem/hvad035); ESA, Advanced ultrasound (https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Human_and_Robotic_Exploration/International_Space_Station_Benefits_for_Humanity/Advanced_Ultrasound_for_the_Space_Program_and_on_Earth); NASA NTRS, AMIS 2024 (https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20240004505/downloads/AMIS_AsMA_2024_final.pdf); NASA NTRS 20160009326, ISS communications (https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20160009326/downloads/20160009326.pdf).

## Crew-11 medical return (January 2026)

As published: in January 2026 NASA ended the Crew-11 mission early and returned the whole Dragon crew to Earth in a controlled medical evacuation, so that a crew member could reach advanced imaging that is not on the station. The medical condition is private; this library does not describe it.

Sources: NPR, 15 January 2026 (https://www.npr.org/2026/01/15/nx-s1-5677647/nasa-spacex-crew-11-splashdown-return); Spaceflight Now, 9 January 2026 (https://spaceflightnow.com/2026/01/09/crew-11-to-cut-mission-short-and-return-to-earth-due-to-medical-issue/).
