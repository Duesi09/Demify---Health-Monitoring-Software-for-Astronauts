# Cabin environment and CO₂

The spacecraft's air, water, temperature, noise, lighting and microbes are all engineered and shared. The hostile, closed environment brings CO₂ headaches, immune changes and microbial risk, stress hormone elevation, and noise and light that disturb sleep.

## Cabin CO₂ limits

- NASA-STD-3001 Vol. 2, V2 6004: cabin CO₂ at or below 3 mmHg as a 1-hour average; above it escalates.
- NASA OCHMO-TB-004: below 2.5 mmHg (below 3,300 ppm) as the 7-day average target. Demify uses it as the nominal band.
- Demify bands (NASA basis): nominal below 2.5 mmHg; caution 2.5–3.0 mmHg; critical above 3.0 mmHg as a 1-hour average.
- Demify does not average: one reading above 3 mmHg counts as a breach and escalates (a prototype simplification). Headache or vision change with high CO₂ is also a reason to contact the flight surgeon.

## Why CO₂ matters in flight

Without convection CO₂ pools around the face. Headache odds roughly double per +1 mmHg (Law 2014, cited in OCHMO-TB-004). CO₂ may contribute to SANS, the eye changes of long-duration flight. Breathing rate rises with cabin CO₂; an elevated rate at rest can be an early CO₂ or infection signal. Cabin CO₂ also disturbs sleep.

## Crew actions when CO₂ is high

1. Move to a well-ventilated module; position a fan.
2. Check CO₂ scrubbers (CDRA).
3. Reduce exercise while CO₂ is high.

Measurement: ECLSS cabin monitors and personal CO₂ monitors. The NASA limit is defined as a 1-hour average; this prototype treats a single high reading as a breach.

## Cabin pressure and oxygen

ISS air is at sea-level pressure, but exploration vehicles plan reduced cabin pressure, so mild hypoxia (confusion, headache, poor sleep) becomes possible. When SpO₂ is low, check the cabin O₂ and CO₂ readings (see "Vital-sign ranges and escalation rules").
